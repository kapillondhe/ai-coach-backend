# Swim Training Plan Structure

## Goal-dependent plan types

"Swim training" covers meaningfully different demands depending on goal, and plan
structure should follow the goal rather than a single generic template:

- **Fitness/technique-focused swimming**: prioritizes efficient stroke mechanics
  and consistent frequency over structured intensity; most improvement for
  newer swimmers comes from technique, not fitness, since swimming is unusually
  technique-limited compared to running or cycling — a technically inefficient
  swimmer can be aerobically fit and still swim slowly.
- **Masters/competitive meet preparation**: requires the full intensity
  spectrum (aerobic base, CSS/threshold, VO2max, sprint) layered on top of
  stroke-specific technique work, periodized toward a specific meet date. See
  `swim-pace-training-zones.md` for the pace zones this training is built
  around.
- **Open-water distance events** (e.g. marathon swims, 5-10km open water races):
  emphasizes sustained aerobic volume and pacing discipline over sprint
  capacity, plus the open-water-specific skills (sighting, drafting, navigating
  chop) covered in `open-water-swim-skills.md`.
- **Triathlon swim leg**: trains the swim as one of three disciplines competing
  for recovery capacity rather than as a standalone goal — see
  `triathlon-training-plan.md` for how swim volume is allocated within a
  multisport build.

## Training phases

- **Technique-first base phase** (first 30-40% of the plan): establishes
  efficient stroke mechanics before adding significant intensity — drilling
  (see `swimming-warmup-and-drills.md`) and easy-paced aerobic volume dominate
  this phase. Swimmers new to structured training, or returning after a long
  break, should spend proportionally longer here than in an equivalent
  running or cycling base phase, since poor technique compounds under fatigue
  and hard sets built on inefficient mechanics reinforce rather than fix the
  problem.
- **Build phase** (middle 30-40%): introduces CSS/threshold sets and, for
  competitive goals, VO2max and sprint work simulating race demands (starts,
  turns, finishes). Stroke-specific and IM (individual medley) work is
  layered in here for competitive swimmers.
- **Peak phase** (2-3 weeks before taper): highest combined volume and
  intensity of the cycle, including race-pace-specific sets at competition
  distance.
- **Taper** (final 1-2 weeks): volume drops 40-60% (often more pronounced in
  swimming than in running or cycling, since accumulated fatigue is
  historically shown to suppress swim performance more than for the other
  two disciplines) while some high-intensity, short-rest work is retained to
  keep "feel for the water" and stroke rate sharp — the same taper logic as
  `marathon-training-plan.md` and `cycling-training-plan.md`, adapted to
  swimming's larger taper-volume drop.

## Weekly structure and set variety

A typical structured week mixes session types rather than repeating the same
set: an aerobic-volume session, a CSS/threshold session, a technique/drill-heavy
session, and (for competitive swimmers) a sprint/speed session, with kick-only
and pull-only sets distributed across sessions to isolate leg and upper-body
contributions respectively. Hard CSS or sprint sessions should be spaced with at
least a day of easier swimming between them, following the same
recovery-spacing logic used in `power-based-training-zones.md` for cycling
intervals.

## Volume progression

Weekly volume (total distance, or time for less-structured swimmers) should
increase by roughly 10% per week, with a down week (volume cut 20-30%) every
third or fourth week — the same progressive-overload and recovery pattern used
in `marathon-training-plan.md`, `cycling-training-plan.md`, and
`triathlon-training-plan.md`. Because swimming is technique-limited, volume
progression should pause (rather than continue on schedule) whenever technique
is visibly breaking down under fatigue, since swimming poor mechanics at high
volume both blunts fitness gains and raises overuse-injury risk (see
`common-tendon-and-joint-conditions.md` for swimmer's shoulder specifically).

## Dryland and strength training

Dryland strength training 2-3x/week — emphasizing scapular stability, rotator
cuff strength, and core/trunk rotation rather than heavy pressing volume —
supports the shoulder through swimming's high repetitive overhead-rotation
demand (competitive distance swimmers accumulate tens of thousands of
strokes/week) and is one of the more consistently evidence-backed ways to
reduce swimmer's shoulder risk. See `swimming-warmup-and-drills.md` for the
dryland warmup routine this complements.

## Recovery and monitoring fatigue

Because swimming is low-impact, volume can climb without the joint-pain
warning signs that often catch overtraining in running — the same pattern
noted for cycling in `cycling-training-plan.md`. Shoulder soreness that
persists into the next session, a drop in stroke-rate efficiency at a given
pace, and declining performance on a repeated benchmark set (e.g. a monthly
CSS retest) are the more relevant early warning signs for swimmers
specifically; see `recovery-and-overtraining.md` for general fatigue-monitoring
principles.
