# Marathon Training Plan Structure

## Training phases

A marathon build typically runs 16-20 weeks and progresses through four phases:

- **Base phase** (first 4-6 weeks): builds aerobic volume at easy effort, establishing
  the mileage foundation the rest of the plan will load on top of. No speedwork yet.
- **Build phase** (middle 6-10 weeks): introduces marathon-pace runs, tempo runs, and
  longer intervals while weekly mileage keeps climbing. This is where most fitness
  gains happen.
- **Peak phase** (2-3 weeks before taper): highest weekly mileage and longest long
  runs of the cycle, including the one or two runs at or near race distance (18-22
  miles / 29-35 km).
- **Taper** (final 2-3 weeks): weekly mileage drops roughly 20-25% per week while
  intensity is maintained, so the runner arrives at the start line recovered but not
  detrained (see below).

Runners with less than a consistent 4-6 week aerobic base before starting a marathon
plan are at meaningfully higher injury risk and should extend the base phase rather
than compress the build phase to hit an arbitrary race date.

## Weekly mileage progression

Increase total weekly mileage by no more than about 10% per week, and insert a
"down week" (mileage cut 20-30% below the prior week) every third or fourth week to
let the body absorb the training load. This down-week pattern reduces overuse-injury
risk without meaningfully slowing fitness gains, since most adaptation happens during
recovery rather than during the loading week itself.

Long-run distance should not exceed about 30-40% of total weekly mileage — a long run
disproportionate to weekly volume increases injury risk and produces a fatigue hole
that compromises the rest of that week's training.

## Long runs

The long run is the single most important marathon-specific session: it trains
glycogen storage and utilization, fat oxidation, musculoskeletal durability for
time-on-feet, and race-day fueling/hydration logistics. Most long runs should be run
at an easy, conversational effort (roughly 60-75% of max heart rate); inserting
marathon-goal-pace miles into the back half of a long run (e.g. the last 3-6 miles of
an 18-mile run) once every 2-3 weeks during the build phase teaches the body to hold
goal pace on tired legs, which is closer to actual race conditions than a fresh-legs
tempo run.

## Taper

Tapering reduces training volume while preserving some intensity, allowing
accumulated fatigue to dissipate before race day without losing fitness. A well-run
taper:

- Cuts weekly mileage by roughly 20-25% per week over the final 2-3 weeks (e.g. a
  50-mile peak week tapers toward ~38, then ~28 miles).
- Keeps some short, race-pace or slightly faster intervals in the mix (e.g.
  4-6 x 1 mile at goal pace) so legs stay sharp — cutting intensity along with volume
  leads to feeling flat and heavy on race day.
- Should not include any new or unusually long/hard efforts; the taper window is for
  consolidating fitness already built, not adding more.

Runners commonly feel sluggish or doubt their fitness partway through a taper as
accumulated fatigue is still clearing — this is a normal, well-documented phenomenon
and not a sign of lost fitness.

## Injury-risk flags during a build

Persistent (not just first-mile) joint pain, a sharp pain that changes gait, or pain
that worsens within a single run rather than warming up are signals to cut a run
short and take 2-3 easy or rest days before resuming, rather than running through it.
See `knee-safe-substitutions.md` and `shoulder-safe-substitutions.md` in the
exercises domain for cross-training substitutions that maintain fitness around a
flare-up.
