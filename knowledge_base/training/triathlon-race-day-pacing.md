# Triathlon Race-Day Pacing and Transitions

## Overall pacing philosophy

Triathlon pacing is about managing effort across three sequential disciplines
that draw on overlapping muscle groups and energy systems, not pacing each
discipline as if it were a standalone race. The most common execution error,
at every distance, is over-biking relative to run fitness — hammering the
bike segment feels rewarding in the moment but degrades run pace far more
than the bike time saved, especially past Olympic distance.

## Swim leg

- Start 5-10 seconds/100m slower than goal pace for the first 200-300m to
  avoid the anaerobic spike caused by mass/wave starts, then settle into
  sustainable rhythm.
- Draft directly behind or on the hip of another swimmer's feet when legal
  and safe to do so — drafting in open water can reduce swim energy cost by
  roughly 10-25% for minimal pace cost, a larger effect than most first-time
  triathletes exploit.
- Sight (lift eyes briefly to spot the next buoy) every 6-10 strokes rather
  than every stroke — over-sighting breaks rhythm and adds meaningfully to
  swim time over race distance.

## T1 (swim-to-bike)

- Practice wetsuit removal (unzip and peel to the waist while still running
  to the bike, finish removing at the bike rack) in training — a fumbled
  wetsuit strip is one of the most common avoidable time losses for
  first-time wetsuit-legal racers.
- Lay out helmet, sunglasses, and bike shoes in a fixed, rehearsed order
  beforehand; mentally rehearsing the T1 sequence reduces the decision-making
  slowdown common in a first triathlon.
- Fasten the helmet before touching the bike — most race organizations
  disqualify athletes for handling the bike with an unfastened helmet, and
  officials do enforce this at the rack.

## Bike leg

- Pace by power (if a power meter is available) or heart rate rather than
  speed, since speed is heavily affected by wind, drafting (where legal), and
  course terrain in ways power/HR are not.
- A commonly cited target is to hold bike effort at or below roughly 75-85%
  of functional threshold power (or the Zone 3/tempo heart-rate band from
  `heart-rate-training-zones.md`) for 70.3/full-distance racing, reserving
  higher effort for sprint/Olympic where the run leg is short enough to
  tolerate more bike fatigue.
- On hilly or technical courses, target normalized power (an estimate that
  weights the variability of effort) rather than average power/speed — riding
  evenly to a normalized-power target produces a faster overall time than
  surging on climbs and coasting on descents to hit the same average speed.
- Fuel and hydrate primarily on the bike (see
  `triathlon-race-fueling.md`) — it is the easiest leg in which to eat and
  drink, and under-fueling here creates a nutrition deficit the run leg
  cannot make up.

## T2 (bike-to-run)

- Rack the bike, swap shoes, and grab run-specific nutrition/hat/visor in a
  practiced sequence; T2 is typically faster than T1 (no wetsuit) but still
  benefits from rehearsal.
- Leave the bike computer/watch lap-split at T2 start so early run pace can
  be monitored — the first mile off the bike is the single easiest place to
  misjudge effort due to brick-leg sensation and adrenaline.

## Run leg (off the bike)

- Expect "brick legs" — a heavy, stiff, sometimes uncoordinated sensation for
  the first 5-15 minutes off the bike — as normal rather than a sign of poor
  fitness or an under-fueled bike leg; effort should feel controlled even
  though the pace will feel slower than the same effort in an open run.
- Target a negative-split or even-split run rather than starting at goal
  race pace immediately off the bike — starting conservatively for the first
  1-2km/1 mile lets the legs adapt and consistently produces a faster overall
  run time than an aggressive start that fades.
- Because run pace off the bike is typically 10-20+ seconds/km slower than
  open-run pace at the same perceived effort or heart rate, athletes should
  set run goals from brick-workout data (see `triathlon-training-plan.md`)
  rather than from open 5k/10k times alone.

## Distance-specific effort allocation

- **Sprint/Olympic**: closer to an all-out effort across all three legs,
  since total race duration (1-3 hours) allows less margin for pacing error
  before fatigue becomes limiting, and recovery time post-race is short.
- **70.3**: bike and run pacing should leave a clear aerobic reserve — most
  athletes' biggest 70.3 pacing mistake is treating the bike leg like an
  Olympic-distance effort.
- **Full-distance**: an even more conservative bike effort (see the
  75-85% FTP guidance above, often at the low end of that range) is standard,
  since the marathon-distance run leg is unforgiving of excess bike fatigue
  and a positive-split or walk-heavy run is the most common full-distance
  race-day failure mode.
