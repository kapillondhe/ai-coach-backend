# Strength Training for Marathon Runners

## Why runners need strength training

Strength training improves running economy (the energy cost of holding a given
pace) by roughly 2-8% in trained runners, comparable in effect size to several weeks
of pure aerobic training, and is one of the most consistently effective tools for
reducing overuse-injury rates during a marathon build. It does not meaningfully add
bulk or hurt endurance performance at the volumes runners typically use (1-2
sessions/week) — the concern that lifting will "slow runners down" is not supported
by the evidence at that frequency.

## Key movement patterns

A runner-focused strength program prioritizes:

- **Single-leg strength** (step-ups, split squats, single-leg Romanian deadlifts):
  running is a single-leg activity, so single-leg strength transfers more directly
  than bilateral lifts and also addresses the side-to-side strength asymmetries that
  drive many overuse injuries.
- **Hip abductor/external rotator strength** (banded lateral walks, side-lying leg
  raises, single-leg bridges): controls the inward knee drift implicated in IT band
  syndrome and patellofemoral pain (see `running-injury-prevention.md`).
- **Calf and ankle strength** (standing and seated calf raises, single-leg calf
  raises progressing to weighted): the calf-Achilles complex absorbs several times
  bodyweight in force per stride; undertrained calves are a common contributor to
  Achilles tendinopathy and shin splints.
- **Core/trunk stability** (planks, dead bugs, anti-rotation presses): maintains
  pelvis and trunk position late in a run as fatigue sets in, when running form
  typically degrades most.

## Programming within a marathon build

- **Base phase**: 2 sessions/week, moderate load, building a strength foundation
  before running volume peaks.
- **Build/peak phase**: drop to 1-2 sessions/week, lighter and more maintenance-
  focused, scheduled the same day as or the day after a hard running session rather
  than the day before, so leg fatigue from lifting doesn't compromise key runs.
- **Taper**: continue light strength work at reduced volume through race week —
  stopping strength training entirely during the taper is unnecessary and can leave
  runners feeling "flat," similar to over-tapering running volume.

## Plyometrics

Low-volume plyometric work (e.g. skips, bounds, low box jumps, 1-2 sets of 5-8 reps)
improves tendon stiffness and running economy, but should be introduced during the
base phase — not the peak or taper — and kept low-volume throughout, since
plyometric loading recovers more slowly than most runners expect and can compound
with high running mileage to increase injury risk if overdone.
