# Common Marathon-Training Injuries and Prevention

## Why marathon training carries elevated injury risk

Marathon training injuries are overwhelmingly overuse injuries (repetitive
sub-maximal loading accumulating faster than tissue can adapt) rather than acute
trauma. The two biggest modifiable risk factors are rapid increases in weekly
mileage and long runs disproportionate to weekly volume (see
`marathon-training-plan.md`); the two biggest non-modifiable risk factors are prior
injury history and low bone density. Roughly half of marathon trainees report at
least one training-limiting injury per cycle, most commonly at the knee, lower leg,
or foot.

## IT band syndrome

Pain on the outside of the knee, usually appearing a few miles into a run and
worsening with continued running (unlike joint pain, which often worsens
immediately). Caused by irritation where the iliotibial band crosses the lateral
femoral condyle, typically driven by weak hip abductors (gluteus medius) allowing
the knee to drift inward on each stride rather than by band "tightness" itself.
Prevention/management centers on hip-abductor strengthening (side-lying leg raises,
banded lateral walks, single-leg bridges) rather than IT band stretching or foam
rolling alone, which give short-term symptom relief but don't address the underlying
strength deficit.

## Plantar fasciitis

Sharp heel/arch pain, classically worst with the first steps in the morning or after
sitting, easing somewhat with continued walking. Driven by repetitive strain on the
plantar fascia, often from a sudden mileage or intensity increase, inadequate calf
flexibility, or a sudden change in footwear. Early-stage management: calf and
plantar-fascia stretching, foot-intrinsic strengthening (towel scrunches, short-foot
exercises), and temporarily reducing mileage; a persistent case (6+ weeks despite
these) warrants evaluation for a night splint, orthotics, or physical therapy rather
than continuing to run through it, since it can become chronic.

## Shin splints (medial tibial stress syndrome)

Diffuse ache along the inner shin, most common early in a training cycle when
mileage or intensity ramps up faster than bone and connective tissue can adapt.
Distinguish from a tibial stress fracture: shin splints produce diffuse pain over a
several-inch area that eases with rest between runs, while a stress fracture
produces a specific, pinpoint tender spot that tends to worsen even with rest and
can hurt with hopping on one leg — a suspected stress fracture needs medical
evaluation and a period of non-impact cross-training, not just reduced mileage.
Shin splint prevention: conservative mileage progression, running-shoe rotation, and
calf/tibialis-anterior strengthening.

## Patellofemoral pain ("runner's knee")

Diffuse ache around or behind the kneecap, worse with downhill running, stairs, or
prolonged sitting with the knee bent. Driven by imbalanced quad/hip strength
altering kneecap tracking within its groove, not by the joint itself wearing out.
Responds well to quad and hip strengthening (see `strength-training-for-runners.md`)
combined with a temporary reduction in downhill running and running volume; it is
one of the more common injuries that resolves fully with a strength-focused approach
rather than rest alone.

## Return-to-running after an overuse injury

Return only once pain-free with daily activities and, for bone/tendon injuries, pain
-free with single-leg hopping. Resume at roughly 50% of pre-injury volume and
progress at half the normal 10%/week rate for 2-3 weeks before returning to full
progression — resuming at full pre-injury mileage immediately is the most common
cause of re-injury. See `knee-safe-substitutions.md` (exercises domain) for
cross-training options that maintain aerobic fitness during the reduced-impact
return window.
