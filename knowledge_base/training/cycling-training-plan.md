# Cycling Training Plan Structure

## Goal-dependent plan types

Unlike running, "cycling training" covers meaningfully different demands
depending on goal, and plan structure should follow the goal rather than a single
generic template:

- **Endurance/century (100mi/160km) riding**: prioritizes time-in-saddle and
  aerobic durability over high-intensity work; plans lean heavily toward Zone 2
  volume (see `power-based-training-zones.md`) with a long ride each week that
  progressively extends toward goal distance.
- **Road racing/criteriums**: requires the full intensity spectrum, since races
  are decided by repeated hard efforts (attacks, climbs, sprints) layered on top
  of a high aerobic base — VO2max and anaerobic-capacity work matter more here
  than for pure endurance goals.
- **Time trial/triathlon bike leg**: rewards a high sustainable threshold power
  and aerodynamic position discipline over surges or sprint capacity; training
  concentrates on threshold and sweet-spot work at goal race position. See
  `triathlon-training-plan.md` for how the bike leg fits within a multisport
  build specifically.
- **Indoor structured training (e.g. off-season)**: allows precise power-zone
  execution without terrain/wind variability, and is well suited to shorter,
  more tightly targeted interval sessions than outdoor riding permits.

## Training phases

- **Base phase** (first 30-40% of the plan): builds aerobic volume in Zone 2,
  develops pedaling technique and cadence efficiency, and for racing- or
  event-specific goals, is the appropriate place to introduce structured
  strength training (see `strength-training-for-runners.md` for general
  principles — a cyclist's emphasis shifts toward posterior-chain and core
  strength that supports sustained aerodynamic position rather than
  running-specific patterns).
- **Build phase** (middle 30-40%): introduces sweet-spot and threshold
  intervals, and for racing goals, VO2max and anaerobic work simulating race
  demands (attacks, climbs, surges). Weekly volume and intensity both climb
  through this phase.
- **Peak phase** (2-3 weeks before taper): highest combined volume and
  intensity of the cycle, including the longest goal-specific efforts (e.g. a
  near-goal-distance long ride, or race-simulation intervals).
- **Taper** (final 1-2 weeks): volume drops 40-60% while some high-intensity
  work is retained at reduced duration, keeping legs sharp without carrying
  fatigue into the event — the same taper logic used in
  `marathon-training-plan.md`, applied to cycling's higher tolerance for
  frequent short, sharp efforts even late in the taper.

## Weekly structure

A typical structured week places 1-2 hard interval sessions (threshold,
sweet-spot, or VO2max depending on phase and goal) early enough in the week to
allow 1-2 full recovery or easy days before the next hard effort, with the
long endurance ride usually on a weekend day when more time is available.
Back-to-back hard days should be avoided outside of deliberate race-simulation
blocks late in a build, since threshold/VO2max work depletes glycogen and
taxes the nervous system more than easy Zone 2 volume of the same duration.

## Volume progression

Weekly volume (hours or training stress, not just distance, since climbing
and wind load the body differently than flat riding at the same distance)
should increase by roughly 10% per week, with a down week (volume cut 20-30%)
every third or fourth week — the same progressive-overload and recovery
pattern used in `marathon-training-plan.md` and `triathlon-training-plan.md`.
Riders returning from time off or new to structured training should spend
several weeks establishing consistent Zone 2 volume before adding
structured intervals, since threshold-level efforts on an undeveloped
aerobic base produce disproportionate fatigue for the fitness gained.

## Cadence and pedaling technique

Cadence preference varies by rider and terrain (typically 80-100rpm on flat
terrain, lower on steep climbs), but training a range of cadences —
including short low-cadence, high-force intervals (e.g. 50-60rpm) and
high-cadence, low-force intervals (e.g. 100-110rpm) — builds both muscular
strength and neuromuscular efficiency, rather than reinforcing a single
habitual cadence that may not be optimal in all riding conditions (climbing,
sprinting, time-trialing).

## Recovery and monitoring fatigue

Cycling's low impact relative to running means volume can climb faster
before overuse injury appears, which paradoxically makes it easier to
overreach on fatigue without the joint-pain warning signs that often catch
overtraining in runners. See `recovery-and-overtraining.md` for general
fatigue-monitoring principles; elevated resting heart rate, declining power
at a given perceived effort, and persistent leg heaviness into what should be
an easy ride are the more relevant early warning signs for cyclists
specifically.
