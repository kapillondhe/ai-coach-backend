# Recovery, Sleep, and Overtraining in Marathon Training

## Recovery is where fitness gains actually happen

Training provides the stimulus, but the aerobic and musculoskeletal adaptations
(increased mitochondrial density, capillarization, tendon/bone remodeling) occur
during recovery between sessions, not during the sessions themselves. This is why
periodized plans build in down weeks and rest days (see `marathon-training-plan.md`)
rather than maximizing mileage every week — a plan without planned recovery
undermines the adaptation it's trying to produce.

## Sleep

Endurance athletes in heavy training typically need 7-9+ hours per night, with elite
endurance athletes commonly targeting 8-10 hours including naps during peak-volume
weeks. Sleep restriction measurably impairs glycogen storage, reaction time, and
perceived effort at a given pace, and is associated with higher illness and injury
rates in athletes under high training load. Runners who cut sleep during peak
mileage weeks to fit in early-morning runs are working against the exact recovery
process the extra mileage depends on.

## Signs of functional overreaching vs. overtraining

Short-term functional overreaching (a few days of degraded performance and elevated
fatigue after a hard training block) is a normal, even intentional, part of
periodized training and typically resolves within a few days to a week of reduced
load. Non-functional overreaching or overtraining syndrome is a more serious,
longer-lasting state and is suggested by:

- Performance decline that persists for 2+ weeks despite reduced training.
- Elevated resting heart rate (5+ bpm above normal baseline) or suppressed heart-rate
  response to hard efforts.
- Persistent fatigue, mood disturbance, or sleep disruption despite adequate sleep
  opportunity.
- Frequent minor illness or unusually slow recovery from small injuries.

Athletes showing these signs need a genuine reduction in training load (days to
weeks, not one easy day) and should not attempt to train through it, since
overtraining syndrome can take months to fully resolve if pushed further.

## Deload and recovery-week principles

A recovery week (mileage cut 20-40% below the prior 2-3 weeks, intensity reduced or
removed) every 3-4 weeks during a marathon build lets accumulated fatigue clear
before it compounds into non-functional overreaching. Recovery weeks are not
"wasted" training time — most measurable fitness improvement in a training block
is detectable only after the subsequent recovery period, a phenomenon often called
the delayed training effect.

## Active recovery and rest days

Complete rest and light active recovery (easy cycling, swimming, walking) produce
similar recovery benefit for most runners; active recovery's main advantage is
maintaining movement habits and blood flow without added impact loading, not a
physiological recovery advantage over full rest. At least one full rest or
active-recovery day per week is standard even at peak marathon mileage.
