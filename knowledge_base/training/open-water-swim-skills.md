# Open-Water Swim Skills for Triathlon

## Why open water differs from pool swimming

Pool fitness does not automatically transfer to open water: there are no
lane lines to swim straight, no wall for orientation or push-offs, variable
visibility and chop, and a mass or wave start with significant incidental
contact. Athletes who only train in a pool should plan for at least 4-6
open-water sessions (in a wetsuit if the race will be wetsuit-legal) before
race day, ideally including at least one simulated mass start.

## Sighting

Sighting is lifting the eyes just above the waterline to spot a buoy or
landmark without fully lifting the head (which drops the hips and adds drag).

- Time the sighting stroke so the eyes lift on the same side as an existing
  breath, or just before a breath, to minimize added stroke disruption.
- Sight every 6-10 strokes in calm water, more frequently (every 3-5 strokes)
  in chop or when navigation is harder to judge, and dial back once the
  athlete has established they are tracking straight.
- Practice sighting in training pool sessions (lifting eyes to a fixed point
  at the end of the lane) so the movement is automatic before race day —
  first-time sighting under race-day adrenaline tends to be overdone.

## Drafting

Swimming close behind (in the "wash" of the feet) or beside the hip of
another swimmer reduces the drafting swimmer's energy cost by roughly
10-25% for a similar pace, one of the largest legal free performance gains
available in triathlon swimming.

- Drafting off feet directly in front works best when the pace matches
  closely enough to hold position without repeatedly closing distance and
  colliding.
- Hip-drafting (swimming just behind and to the side of another athlete's
  hip) avoids the turbulence and unpredictable kicking of drafting directly
  behind feet, at a slightly smaller but still meaningful energy benefit.
- Position at the start (e.g. starting near strong swimmers of a similar
  pace) meaningfully affects how much of the race can be spent drafting —
  starting too far back or beside much slower swimmers forecloses the option
  for the first several hundred meters.

## Wetsuit use

- Wetsuits add buoyancy (particularly at the hips and legs), which improves
  body position and reduces drag for most triathletes, typically improving
  swim time even accounting for slightly restricted shoulder mobility.
  Practicing in the wetsuit beforehand matters because the shoulder
  restriction changes stroke feel and cadence.
- Wetsuit legality is typically governed by water temperature (exact cutoffs
  vary by sanctioning body and race, often prohibited above roughly 76-84°F
  /24-29°C and mandatory below a lower threshold around 60-64°F/16-18°C) —
  check the specific race's rules rather than assuming.
- Practice the removal sequence (unzip, peel to the waist while exiting the
  water and running to T1, finish stripping at the rack) — see
  `triathlon-race-day-pacing.md` for the T1 sequence this feeds into.

## Managing mass-start anxiety ("washing machine" starts)

Contact, kicked goggles, and a feeling of being unable to find a breathing
rhythm are common in the first few hundred meters of a mass or wave start,
and are a leading cause of panic or DNFs among newer open-water swimmers
despite adequate fitness.

- Starting toward the back or side of a wave (rather than the front-center,
  where contact is densest) trades a small amount of drafting/positioning
  opportunity for meaningfully less early contact — a reasonable trade for
  athletes newer to mass starts.
- If panic or breathlessness sets in, rolling briefly onto the back or
  switching to breaststroke for a few strokes to regain a breathing rhythm is
  a normal, allowed adjustment, not a sign of failure — trying to power
  through a panic response by swimming harder often worsens it.
- Simulating race-morning conditions in at least one training swim (cold
  water, wetsuit, some incidental contact from training partners) reduces the
  novelty that drives race-day anxiety.
