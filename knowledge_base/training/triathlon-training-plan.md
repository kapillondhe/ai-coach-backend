# Triathlon Training Plan Structure

## Race distances and typical training windows

- **Sprint** (750m swim / 20km bike / 5km run): 8-12 week plans, 5-8 hours/week,
  accessible to athletes coming from a single-sport background.
- **Olympic/standard** (1.5km / 40km / 10km): 12-16 week plans, 7-10 hours/week.
- **70.3/half-iron** (1.9km / 90km / 21.1km): 16-20 week plans, 8-12 hours/week,
  requiring at least one prior sprint or Olympic finish for most age-groupers.
- **Full-distance/iron** (3.8km / 180km / 42.2km): 20-30 week plans, 10-16+
  hours/week, with a strong recommendation of a completed 70.3 in the prior 12
  months before attempting a full build.

Athletes without a consistent base of swim, bike, and run training (roughly
2-3 sessions/week in each discipline) before starting a distance-specific plan
should spend 4-6 weeks establishing that base rather than starting the
distance plan cold — the base phase below assumes some existing fitness in
all three disciplines, not zero.

## Training phases

- **Base phase** (first 25-35% of the plan): builds aerobic volume across all
  three disciplines at easy effort and establishes technique (especially
  swim), with no race-pace-specific work yet.
- **Build phase** (middle 35-45%): introduces race-pace intervals on the bike
  and run, longer swim sets with race-pace segments, and the first brick
  workouts (see below). Weekly volume keeps climbing through this phase.
- **Peak phase** (2-3 weeks before taper): highest weekly volume and the
  longest/hardest brick sessions of the cycle, simulating race-day
  discipline transitions and duration as closely as training time allows.
- **Taper** (final 1-3 weeks, scaling with race distance — 1 week for
  sprint/Olympic, 2-3 weeks for 70.3/full): volume drops while some
  race-pace intensity is retained, mirroring the marathon taper pattern (see
  `marathon-training-plan.md`) but applied across all three disciplines
  simultaneously.

## Balancing swim/bike/run volume

Unlike single-sport training, weekly hours must be split across three
disciplines competing for the same recovery capacity. A common allocation by
time (not distance) for age-group athletes:

- **Bike**: roughly 45-50% of total training time — it carries the largest
  share of overall race duration (especially 70.3/full) and is the
  lowest-impact discipline, so it can absorb the most volume.
- **Run**: roughly 25-30% of total training time — the highest injury-risk
  discipline per hour trained (highest impact, and often trained on
  already-fatigued legs), so volume here should grow more conservatively than
  bike or swim volume.
- **Swim**: roughly 20-25% of total training time — technique-limited more
  than fitness-limited for most age-groupers, so frequency (3+ sessions/week)
  matters more than single-session duration.

Weekly volume increases should follow roughly the same 10%-per-week ceiling
used in single-sport plans, applied to total training time rather than to
each discipline independently, with a down week (volume cut 20-30%) every
third or fourth week.

## Brick workouts

A "brick" is a bike-to-run (or, less commonly, swim-to-bike) session performed
back-to-back with minimal transition time, training the body to run
efficiently on legs that have just been loaded by cycling — a sensation
("brick legs" or heavy, stiff-feeling legs in the first 5-10 minutes off the
bike) that does not fully replicate in separate sessions.

- Start bricks short (e.g. 30-45 min bike + 10-15 min run) early in the build
  phase and lengthen the run portion as the plan progresses toward peak.
- The run segment should typically be shorter than a standalone run at the
  same point in the plan — the training purpose is transition adaptation, not
  additional run volume, and running long off tired legs disproportionately
  raises injury risk.
- Race-week or peak-phase bricks that approximate goal race pace on both
  legs are more specific and valuable than higher-volume but easy-effort
  bricks, but should still be limited to once every 1-2 weeks to manage
  fatigue and injury risk.

## Strength training and injury prevention

Strength training 2x/week (see `strength-training-for-runners.md` for
generally-applicable guidance, though a triathlete's needs skew toward
shoulder/core stability for swimming in addition to running-relevant
lower-body work) supports durability across the higher combined training
volume triathlon requires relative to single-sport training. See
`running-injury-prevention.md` and `recovery-and-overtraining.md` for signs
of accumulating fatigue that warrant a cutback — these apply across all three
disciplines, not just running, and multisport athletes can mask overall
fatigue by rotating which discipline they push hardest in a given week.
