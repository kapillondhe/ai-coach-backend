# Power-Based Training Zones for Cycling

## Why cycling favors power over heart rate

Power output (watts) responds instantly to effort and is unaffected by heat,
caffeine, hydration, or accumulated fatigue the way heart rate is (see
`heart-rate-training-zones.md`) — a rider's heart rate drifts upward over a long
ride or hot day at a constant power output, which makes heart rate a lagging and
sometimes misleading intensity signal for cycling specifically. A power meter gives
an immediate, objective number, which is why structured cycling training is usually
built around power zones anchored to Functional Threshold Power (FTP) rather than
heart-rate zones, even though heart rate remains a useful secondary data point for
tracking fatigue and cardiac drift within a ride.

## Functional Threshold Power (FTP)

FTP is the highest average power a rider can sustain for roughly one hour, and
serves as the anchor for every zone below. It is most commonly estimated from a
20-minute all-out test (average power x 0.95, since most riders can hold slightly
more than their true hour-power for 20 minutes) or a ramp test to exhaustion; a
true 60-minute time-trial effort is more accurate but rarely practical to repeat
often. FTP should be retested every 6-8 weeks during a structured block, since
training zones calculated from a stale FTP under- or overshoot actual fitness and
distort every subsequent workout's intended effort.

## Zone definitions (7-zone model, % of FTP)

- **Zone 1 — Active recovery (under 55%)**: barely-there pedaling used to promote
  blood flow on rest days or between hard intervals; should feel almost too easy.
- **Zone 2 — Endurance (55-75%)**: the aerobic-base workhorse zone, fully
  conversational, where the large majority of season and base-phase volume belongs
  — directly analogous to Zone 2 in the heart-rate model.
- **Zone 3 — Tempo (75-90%)**: a moderately hard, sustainable-for-hours effort;
  useful but easy to overuse as a substitute for genuine easy riding, the same
  "gray zone" trap described in `heart-rate-training-zones.md`.
- **Zone 4 — Threshold (90-105%)**: effort at or near FTP itself, sustainable for
  roughly 20-60 minutes in intervals; the primary zone for raising FTP directly.
- **Zone 5 — VO2max (106-120%)**: sustainable for 3-8 minutes per interval, raises
  aerobic ceiling and is typically programmed as repeats (e.g. 5 x 4 minutes) with
  substantial recovery between.
- **Zone 6 — Anaerobic capacity (121-150%)**: sustainable for 30 seconds to 3
  minutes, builds short, hard-effort capacity relevant to attacks, hard climbs, and
  bridging gaps in a race.
- **Zone 7 — Neuromuscular power (maximal, brief)**: sprint efforts of a few
  seconds, limited by neuromuscular recruitment rather than aerobic capacity.

## Sweet spot training

"Sweet spot" (roughly 88-94% of FTP, the upper end of tempo bleeding into
threshold) is a popular middle ground for time-constrained riders: it produces
much of the fitness benefit of full threshold work with a lower fatigue and
recovery cost, allowing more total quality volume per week than threshold
intervals alone would permit. It is not a replacement for true threshold and
VO2max work in a build phase aimed at a specific event, but is an efficient
default for riders fitting structured training around limited weekly hours.

## Programming zones across a training week

- Most structured cycling weeks follow the same polarized pattern described in
  `heart-rate-training-zones.md`: 75-85% of volume in Zone 2 or below, with 1-2
  dedicated hard sessions (threshold, sweet spot, or VO2max intervals) per week
  and the rest of the week's riding kept genuinely easy.
- Hard interval sessions should be spaced with at least one easy or rest day
  between them — threshold and VO2max work draws heavily on the same glycogen and
  neuromuscular systems, and stacking them without recovery blunts the quality of
  the second session and raises overtraining risk (see
  `recovery-and-overtraining.md`).

## Power-to-weight and outdoor variability

FTP in absolute watts matters for flat, high-speed riding, but power-to-weight
(watts/kg) is the better predictor of climbing performance — a lighter rider with
a lower absolute FTP can out-climb a heavier rider with higher absolute FTP at the
same watts/kg. Outdoors, wind, drafting, and terrain make target power harder to
hold steady than on an indoor trainer; riders newer to power-based training often
find it easier to learn pacing discipline on a trainer before applying strict power
targets to variable outdoor terrain.
