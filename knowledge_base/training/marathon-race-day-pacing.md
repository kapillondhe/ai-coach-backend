# Marathon Race-Day Pacing

## Even or negative splits beat positive splits

Runners who run the second half of a marathon at the same pace or slightly faster
than the first half (an even or negative split) consistently finish faster relative
to their fitness than runners who go out too fast and slow down (a positive split).
Starting even 10-15 seconds/mile faster than goal pace in the first few miles is a
common, avoidable cause of a hard late-race fade, since the marathon's aerobic and
glycogen demands punish an early deficit far more than they reward an early surplus.

A practical target: run the first 5k slightly slower (5-10 sec/km or ~10-15 sec/mile)
than goal pace to offset race-morning adrenaline, settle into goal pace through the
middle miles, and hold or gradually pick up effort (not necessarily pace, if terrain
or fatigue works against it) in the final 10k.

## Effort vs. pace

Target pace should be treated as a guide, not a mandate that ignores conditions.
Heat, humidity, wind, hills, and altitude all raise the effort cost of a given pace;
runners who rigidly chase goal pace under harder-than-planned conditions typically
blow up in the final miles, while runners who adjust to hold goal *effort* instead
finish more consistently, even if the finishing time is a few minutes slower than a
flat-course, ideal-weather goal.

## Course-specific adjustments

- **Hills**: run climbs by effort, not pace — pace naturally drops on the way up and
  recovers on the way down. Trying to hold flat-course pace uphill burns
  disproportionate glycogen early.
- **Heat/humidity**: for each 5°C (9°F) above about 15°C (59°F) wet-bulb-adjacent
  conditions, expect meaningful pace slowdown even at the same effort; slow the goal
  pace target rather than the effort target, and increase fluid/electrolyte intake
  (see race-day fueling in the nutrition domain).
- **Net downhill courses**: still require pacing discipline — the eccentric
  quad-loading from sustained downhill running causes cumulative muscle damage that
  can produce a late-race fade just as severe as going out too fast on a flat course.

## The "wall" and how pacing prevents it

Hitting "the wall" (a severe, sudden performance drop, usually 18-22 miles / 29-35 km
in) is most commonly caused by glycogen depletion compounded by starting too fast.
Even, effort-matched pacing combined with adequate in-race carbohydrate intake (see
`marathon-fueling.md` in the nutrition domain) is the most reliable way to avoid it;
by contrast, no amount of race-day fueling fully compensates for a poorly paced first
half.
