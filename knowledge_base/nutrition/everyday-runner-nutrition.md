# Everyday Nutrition for Marathon Training (Non-Race-Day)

## Carbohydrate periodization across a training week

Rather than eating the same carbohydrate intake every day, matching carb intake to
that day's training demand improves both performance and adaptation:

- **Easy/rest days**: lower end of the general endurance-athlete range (see
  `macronutrient-basics.md`), since glycogen demand is low and some sessions
  benefit from being done in a lower-carb-availability state to enhance fat-
  oxidation adaptations.
- **Long-run and hard-session days**: higher end of the range, with extra
  carbohydrate concentrated in the meals immediately before and after the session to
  fuel it and rapidly replace what was used.
- **Day before a long run or key workout**: a modest carb increase supports
  starting the session with fuller glycogen stores, similar in principle to race-week
  carb loading (`marathon-fueling.md`) but far smaller in magnitude.

## Post-long-run recovery nutrition

Consuming roughly 1.0-1.2 g/kg carbohydrate plus 0.3-0.4 g/kg protein within about
30-60 minutes after a long run or hard session accelerates glycogen resynthesis and
muscle repair compared with delaying the next meal by several hours. This matters
most when two demanding sessions fall within 24 hours of each other or when daily
training volume is high; for a single easy run followed by a full day before the
next session, the timing window matters much less than hitting daily totals.

## Iron

Endurance runners, especially women and vegetarian/vegan athletes, have elevated
iron needs and above-average rates of iron deficiency, partly from footstrike
hemolysis (red blood cell destruction from repeated foot impact) and iron loss in
sweat. Unexplained fatigue, declining performance despite consistent training, or
persistently elevated perceived effort at normal paces warrant checking ferritin
(iron stores) rather than assuming it's just training fatigue; supplementing iron
without a confirmed deficiency is not recommended, since excess iron has its own
health risks.

## Vitamin D and calcium

Both support bone health, which matters directly for stress-fracture risk during
high-mileage marathon training. Runners training predominantly indoors, at northern
latitudes, or during winter months are at higher risk of vitamin D insufficiency;
calcium needs are similar to the general population (about 1000-1200 mg/day) but
become more important to actually meet during high-mileage blocks given the bone
stress of repetitive impact loading.

## Alcohol and recovery

Alcohol impairs glycogen resynthesis, sleep quality (even though it can shorten time
to fall asleep, it reduces restorative deep and REM sleep), and next-day perceived
recovery. Occasional moderate consumption is unlikely to derail an otherwise solid
training plan, but drinking on the evening of a long run or key hard session
measurably blunts the recovery that session's timing was chosen to protect.
