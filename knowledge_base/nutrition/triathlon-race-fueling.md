# Triathlon Race Fueling: Swim, Bike, Run

## How triathlon fueling differs from single-sport fueling

Total race duration (particularly 70.3 and full-distance) can exceed what
most marathon fueling guidance (see `marathon-fueling.md`) is calibrated for,
and the fueling opportunity differs sharply by discipline: the swim leg
offers essentially no fueling opportunity, the bike leg is the easiest
discipline in which to consume solid food and larger fluid volumes, and the
run leg (especially off the bike) has the lowest gut tolerance of the three
due to jostling and blood flow shifting toward working leg muscles.

## Pre-race and morning-of

- Eat a low-fat, low-fiber breakfast of 1-4 g/kg carbohydrate 2-4 hours
  before the swim start, as with marathon race-morning guidance — but
  triathlon mass starts are often earlier (sometimes pre-dawn transition
  setup), so plan wake time to preserve the full digestion window rather than
  compressing it.
- A small, easily digested top-off (e.g. a gel or sports drink) 15-30 minutes
  before the swim start is well tolerated by most athletes and helps offset
  the fact that no further fueling is realistic until T1/the bike leg.

## In-race carbohydrate targets by discipline

- **Swim**: no meaningful fueling; rely on the pre-race meal and morning
  top-off to cover this segment.
- **Bike**: the primary fueling window. Trained athletes can typically target
  60-90 g carbohydrate/hour from a mixed glucose-fructose source (as in
  `marathon-fueling.md`'s in-race guidance), and solid food (bars,
  real food) is best tolerated here rather than on the run, since bike
  posture and lower impact are easier on digestion. Front-load fueling early
  in the bike leg rather than backloading it near T2, since a nutrition
  deficit compounds on the run.
- **Run (off the bike)**: gut tolerance typically drops relative to the bike
  leg, so most athletes shift toward 30-60 g carbohydrate/hour from
  easily-absorbed liquid/gel sources rather than solid food, and rely on
  carbohydrate already banked from bike-leg fueling to cover part of the
  run's energy demand.

## Hydration and electrolytes

- Sweat rate and evaporative cooling differ meaningfully between bike (higher
  airflow, generally better cooling) and run (less airflow, often higher
  core temperature for the same effort) — athletes commonly need to
  deliberately increase fluid/electrolyte intake on the run relative to what
  felt sufficient on the bike, rather than assuming a constant rate across
  both legs.
- As with marathon fueling, drink to thirst as the general rule, with
  athletes who are known "salty sweaters" (visible salt residue on skin/kit)
  supplementing electrolytes proactively rather than reactively, especially
  on hot 70.3/full-distance days where multi-hour sodium losses are largest.
- Race-morning wetsuit and swim-leg time add to total time in
  race-day heat/sun exposure that is easy to under-count when planning
  fluid needs, particularly for full-distance events with mid-morning or
  midday run legs.

## Caffeine

Caffeine (roughly 3-6 mg/kg, commonly delivered via caffeinated gels/chews
in the back half of the bike or during the run) is a well-supported
ergogenic aid for endurance performance and perceived-exertion reduction;
athletes who don't habitually consume caffeine should test tolerance in
training rather than trying it for the first time on race day, since
individual GI and stimulant sensitivity varies.

## Gut training

Race-day nutrition tolerance is trainable, and should be practiced during
brick workouts (see `triathlon-training-plan.md`) that combine bike and run
fueling in sequence — practicing bike-leg fueling alone in training does not
reveal how the gut tolerates that same food once running begins. Never
introduce a new product, flavor, or concentration on race day.

## Distance-specific fueling notes

- **Sprint**: total duration (often under 90 minutes) may not require
  in-race fueling beyond fluids for many athletes; a pre-race top-off is
  often sufficient.
- **Olympic**: light bike-leg fueling (gels/drink) is typical; run-leg
  fueling is optional depending on finish time and heat.
- **70.3**: full bike-leg fueling plan required for most athletes, with
  continued lighter fueling on the run; this is the distance where an
  under-practiced fueling plan most commonly causes a late-race bonk or GI
  distress.
- **Full-distance**: fueling is a first-order performance factor, not a
  secondary concern — total carbohydrate, fluid, and sodium needs over
  10-17+ hours require a rehearsed, distance-specific plan built up over
  long training days rather than improvised on race day.
