# Cycling Endurance and Race Fueling

## How cycling fueling differs from running fueling

The bike is the most fueling-friendly discipline of any endurance sport: low
impact and a stable torso position make eating solid food and drinking larger
fluid volumes far more tolerable than running (see `marathon-fueling.md` and
`triathlon-race-fueling.md` for the run-specific contrast). This means cyclists
can and generally should target the higher end of published carbohydrate-intake
guidance for long efforts, since gut tolerance is rarely the limiting factor it
is on the run.

## In-ride carbohydrate targets

- **Rides under 60-90 minutes at moderate effort**: fluids alone are typically
  sufficient; glycogen stores comfortably cover the energy demand.
- **Rides 90 minutes to 2.5 hours**: 30-60 g carbohydrate/hour from a mixed
  glucose-fructose source (drink mix, gels, or chews) maintains blood glucose
  and delays glycogen depletion.
- **Rides beyond 2.5-3 hours, or any race effort**: 60-90+ g carbohydrate/hour
  is well tolerated by most trained riders given the bike's low-impact
  digestion advantage, and higher intakes (up to ~100-120 g/hour) are
  achievable with a gut-trained rider using a high glucose-to-fructose ratio
  product — this is meaningfully higher than typical run-specific guidance
  because bike-leg digestion tolerance is higher.
- Solid, real food (rice cakes, sandwiches, bananas, bars) is well tolerated
  on the bike at moderate aerobic effort and is worth including on very long
  rides both for carbohydrate and for palatability — riders relying solely on
  sweet gels/drink mix for 5+ hours commonly develop "flavor fatigue" that
  reduces intake right when it matters most.

## Pre-ride fueling

A carbohydrate-focused meal (1-4 g/kg) 2-4 hours before a long ride or race,
following the same digestion-window logic as `marathon-fueling.md`, sets
glycogen stores for the effort ahead. Early-morning grand fondo or race starts
that compress this window benefit from a smaller, faster-digesting
pre-ride snack closer to start time rather than skipping the pre-ride meal
entirely.

## Hydration and electrolytes

- Fluid needs scale with heat, humidity, and effort, but cycling's airflow
  (especially at higher outdoor speeds) provides more evaporative cooling
  than running at a comparable effort, which can mask higher-than-perceived
  sweat losses — riders sometimes under-drink on the bike because they feel
  less overtly hot than they would running at similar exertion.
- Indoor trainer riding removes that airflow-driven cooling almost entirely,
  so indoor sweat rates and fluid needs at a given power output are typically
  much higher than the same effort outdoors — a fan is not just for comfort,
  it meaningfully changes hydration requirements.
- As with other endurance sports, drink to thirst as a general guide, with
  proactive electrolyte supplementation for known "salty sweaters" or hot,
  long events where cumulative sodium loss over several hours is significant.

## Caffeine

Caffeine (roughly 3-6 mg/kg) is well-supported for endurance cycling
performance, most commonly timed for the back half of a long ride or race
when perceived effort typically rises; as with other endurance sports, test
tolerance and preferred delivery method (caffeinated gel/chew vs. pre-ride
coffee) in training rather than on race day.

## Gut training and race-day rehearsal

Because the bike tolerates higher carbohydrate intake than running, the
limiting factor for many riders is training the gut to actually absorb
60-90+ g/hour reliably, not tolerance in an abstract sense — this needs
practice on long training rides at race intensity, not just easy rides,
since gut blood flow and absorption both decrease as effort rises. Never
introduce a new product or a meaningfully higher intake rate for the first
time on race day.

## Event-length notes

- **Short races/criteriums (under ~90 min)**: fluids and modest carbohydrate
  intake are usually sufficient; the effort is too intense and too short for
  fueling to be a limiting factor the way pacing and positioning are.
- **Century rides and long fondos (4-7+ hours)**: fueling is a primary
  determinant of finishing strong, not a secondary concern — under-fueling in
  the first half is the most common cause of a late-ride bonk, and is
  difficult to fully recover from mid-ride once glycogen is significantly
  depleted.
- **Multi-day tours or stage races**: overnight glycogen replenishment
  (adequate carbohydrate in the hours after finishing, continuing into the
  evening meal) matters as much as fueling during the ride itself, since
  starting a subsequent day's ride with incompletely restored glycogen
  compounds fatigue across the event.
