# Warmup, Mobility, and Form Drills for Runners

## Why a dynamic warmup, not static stretching, before running

Static stretching (holding a stretch) immediately before running has been shown to
modestly reduce force production and running economy for a short window afterward,
without meaningfully reducing injury risk. A dynamic warmup — movement-based
mobility that raises tissue temperature and rehearses the ranges of motion running
uses — better prepares the body without that performance cost. Static stretching
still has a place, just after running or in a separate dedicated mobility session,
when the goal is longer-term range-of-motion improvement rather than immediate
performance.

## Pre-run dynamic warmup (5-10 minutes)

A typical sequence, roughly 30-60 seconds or 8-12 reps each side:

- **Leg swings** (front-to-back, then side-to-side): mobilizes hips through the
  running range of motion.
- **Walking lunges with a torso twist**: combines hip-flexor stretch with rotational
  mobility.
- **High knees and butt kicks** (short distance, e.g. 20m): raises heart rate and
  rehearses running-specific hip flexion/knee flexion patterns.
- **Lateral lunges / lateral shuffles**: prepares hip abductors and adductors, which
  static forward running rarely challenges but which stabilize the pelvis during
  running.
- **Ankle circles and calf raises**: primes the ankle/calf-Achilles complex that
  absorbs the highest per-stride forces.

## Strides

4-6 x 15-20 second relaxed accelerations up to roughly 85-95% of sprint effort, with
full recovery between, performed after the dynamic warmup and before a quality
session (intervals, tempo) or occasionally after an easy run. Strides prime
neuromuscular firing rate and running form at speed without the fatigue cost of a
genuine sprint effort, and are one of the more evidence-backed low-cost ways to
maintain running economy and leg speed during a marathon build that's otherwise
dominated by slower aerobic running.

## Hip and ankle mobility for runners

Limited hip extension (the ability to extend the leg behind the body) and limited
ankle dorsiflexion (shin-over-foot range) are common mobility restrictions in
runners who sit for long periods, and both are linked to compensatory movement
patterns implicated in IT band syndrome, plantar fasciitis, and Achilles issues (see
`running-injury-prevention.md` in the training domain). Regular hip-flexor stretching
(e.g. kneeling hip-flexor stretch) and ankle dorsiflexion work (e.g. weighted
knee-to-wall stretch) address these directly; this mobility work is more effective
done separately from running (evening, or a dedicated mobility session) than
squeezed into a pre-run warmup where dynamic movement is more valuable.

## Post-run

Easy walking for a few minutes to let heart rate and breathing settle, followed
optionally by static stretching or foam rolling for comfort — neither has strong
evidence for reducing next-day soreness or injury risk, but neither causes harm and
many runners find it helps with perceived recovery and consistency.
