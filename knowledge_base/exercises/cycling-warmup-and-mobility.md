# Warmup, Mobility, and Injury Prevention for Cyclists

## Why cycling's injury profile differs from running

Cycling is low-impact and non-weight-bearing, which makes acute impact injuries
rare, but the sustained, fixed, forward-flexed position and highly repetitive
pedal stroke instead produce overuse and postural issues concentrated in the
knees, low back, neck, and hands — different tissues than the impact-driven
injuries covered in `running-injury-prevention.md`, and often more sensitive to
bike fit than to training load alone.

## Pre-ride warmup

Cycling's own pedaling motion serves as much of its own warmup, so a dedicated
pre-ride routine is shorter than a runner's dynamic warmup:

- **5-10 minutes of easy spinning** (Zone 1-2, see
  `power-based-training-zones.md`) before any structured intervals, gradually
  raising cadence and effort rather than starting cold into a hard effort.
- **Hip circles and leg swings**: mobilizes the hips through a fuller range
  than the pedal stroke itself uses, which is otherwise a narrow, repetitive
  motion.
- **A few short (10-15 second) build efforts** near the end of the warmup,
  similar in purpose to a runner's strides, prime neuromuscular readiness for
  the first hard interval or race effort.

## Common overuse issues and their usual drivers

- **Anterior knee pain**: most often driven by saddle height that's too low
  (increasing knee flexion and patellofemoral load) or a cadence habitually
  too low for the power being pushed (high per-pedal-stroke force); raising
  saddle height slightly and/or favoring higher cadence at the same power
  often resolves it faster than direct knee-strengthening work alone.
- **Iliotibial (IT) band irritation on the outside of the knee**: often linked
  to cleat position or saddle height causing the knee to track outward
  through the pedal stroke; see `knee-safe-substitutions.md` for general
  knee-load-reduction strategies applicable during a flare while a fit issue
  is addressed.
- **Lower back pain**: commonly a combination of a reach/stack (handlebar
  distance and height) that's too aggressive for the rider's current
  hamstring/hip flexibility and core endurance, compounded by weak core and
  hip-extensor endurance rather than acute injury — building core and
  posterior-chain endurance (planks, bird dogs, hip hinges) alongside a fit
  review addresses this more durably than stretching alone.
- **Neck and upper-back discomfort**: from sustained cervical extension to
  keep the head up in a forward-flexed riding position, particularly on
  aggressive road or time-trial setups; deep neck flexor and upper-back
  strengthening plus periodically varying hand position on longer rides both
  help.
- **Hand/wrist numbness (ulnar or median nerve compression)**: from sustained
  pressure through the handlebars, worsened by a reach that's too long or too
  much weight carried on the hands; padded gloves, handlebar tape/gel, and
  periodically shifting hand position during a ride reduce sustained
  compression, though a persistent, worsening numbness pattern warrants a fit
  review and possibly medical evaluation (see
  `medical-red-flags-requiring-referral.md`).

## Bike fit as the first line of prevention

Because so many cycling overuse issues trace back to position rather than
training load, a professional bike fit (or careful self-fit against standard
saddle-height and reach guidelines) is usually a higher-leverage intervention
than exercise-based treatment alone once a positional issue is suspected —
treating the symptom without correcting a genuine fit problem tends to produce
recurring flare-ups. Any fit change should be introduced gradually (e.g. small
saddle-height adjustments over several rides) rather than all at once, since
the body needs time to adapt to a new position even when the new position is
objectively better.

## Off-bike mobility and strength

- **Hip flexor stretching**: the flexed hip angle held for hours on the bike
  shortens hip flexors over time; regular hip-flexor stretching (as in
  `running-warmup-and-mobility.md`) counteracts this and supports a fuller,
  more efficient pedal stroke.
- **Thoracic spine mobility**: counters the sustained upper-back rounding of
  the riding position and supports a more comfortable, sustainable
  handlebar reach.
- **Core and posterior-chain strength**: see `strength-training-for-runners.md`
  for general principles — for cyclists the emphasis shifts toward
  sustained-endurance core work (planks, dead bugs) and hip-extensor strength
  (bridges, Romanian deadlifts) that supports position and power transfer
  over hours in the saddle, rather than running-specific single-leg patterns.

## Post-ride

Easy spinning to bring effort down gradually, followed by the same hip-flexor
and thoracic mobility work above — most valuable after long rides where
position-related stiffness accumulates, rather than skipped in favor of
stretching muscles that did the most work (quads/hamstrings), which matter
less for cycling-specific recovery than the postural muscles held in a fixed
position for hours.
