# Warmup, Drills, and Technique for Swimmers

## Why swimming is technique-limited

Unlike running or cycling, where fitness is usually the main limiter on
performance for recreational athletes, swimming performance is heavily
constrained by stroke efficiency — a fit swimmer with poor technique will
consistently swim slower and tire faster than a less-fit swimmer with efficient
mechanics, because inefficient technique wastes energy fighting drag rather
than converting it into forward propulsion. This is why drilling and
technique-focused swimming occupy a much larger share of a well-structured swim
plan than the equivalent form work does in running or cycling (see
`swim-training-plan.md`).

## Dryland warmup (5-10 minutes, before entering the water)

- **Arm circles and swings** (forward and backward): raises shoulder tissue
  temperature and rehearses the overhead range of motion swimming uses
  repetitively.
- **Band pull-aparts and external rotations**: activates the scapular
  stabilizers and rotator cuff that support the shoulder through high-volume
  overhead rotation — directly relevant to swimmer's shoulder prevention (see
  `common-tendon-and-joint-conditions.md`).
- **Thoracic spine rotations**: mobilizes the mid-back rotation that
  freestyle and backstroke rely on for an efficient body-roll technique.
- **Leg swings and hip circles**: primes hip mobility for kick technique,
  particularly relevant for swimmers whose kick relies on hip-driven rather
  than knee-driven propulsion.

## In-water warmup and drill progression

A typical in-water warmup starts with 200-400m of easy mixed-stroke swimming to
raise heart rate and settle into rhythm, followed by a drill set before any
main-set intensity. Common technique drills, roughly in order of how
foundational they are:

- **Catch-up drill**: one hand stays extended in front until the other hand
  completes its full stroke and catches up, exaggerating full arm extension
  and timing — useful for correcting a rushed or shortened stroke.
- **Fist drill**: swimming with closed fists removes the hand's surface area,
  forcing greater awareness of forearm-and-hand "catch" and pull, and
  typically feels dramatically different (worse) than normal swimming for
  anyone relying too heavily on hand surface area rather than forearm
  position for propulsion.
- **Single-arm drill**: isolates one arm's stroke path and timing while the
  other stays extended or rests at the side, making asymmetries between the
  dominant and non-dominant arm easier to notice and correct.
- **6-kick switch drill**: 6 kicks on each side in a rotated body position
  before switching arms, reinforcing body-roll timing with the stroke rather
  than swimming flat.
- **Sighting drill**: for open-water-bound swimmers, practicing the sighting
  stroke described in `open-water-swim-skills.md` at easy pace before it's
  needed under race fatigue.

## Common technique faults and fixes

- **Dropped elbow on the catch** (forearm and hand pointing down/back too
  early rather than forward, reducing the effective paddle surface):
  addressed with fist drill and, in dryland work, exercises that reinforce
  early forearm vertical positioning.
- **Crossover at entry** (hand entering the water past the body's centerline
  rather than in line with the shoulder): causes a fishtailing, side-to-side
  motion that wastes energy; a common cue is entering the hand in line with
  the same-side shoulder, not the head's centerline.
- **Over-rotation or under-rotation of the body roll**: too little roll
  limits reach and hip-driven power; too much disrupts a stable kick and
  breathing timing — the 6-kick switch drill is the standard tool for finding
  a workable middle ground.
- **Scissor kick** (legs splitting wide apart, usually compensating for poor
  body position or an asymmetric breathing pattern): adds significant drag;
  usually resolves as body position and breathing timing improve rather than
  through kick drilling alone.

## Post-swim

An easy 100-200m cool-down at very easy pace, followed optionally by the same
dryland shoulder mobility work used in the warmup, helps manage the
accumulated shoulder load from a high-repetition session — of particular
relevance for swimmers logging high weekly volume, where cumulative
overhead-rotation load, not any single session, drives most overuse issues.
