# Sports Anatomy Fundamentals

## Joint types and how they constrain training

- **Ball-and-socket (shoulder, hip)**: largest range of motion of any joint type,
  traded for less inherent bony stability — the shoulder in particular relies on the
  rotator cuff and surrounding musculature for stability rather than bone shape, which
  is why rotator-cuff strength matters more for shoulder health than for most other
  joints.
- **Hinge (knee, elbow)**: primarily flexion/extension in one plane; the knee also
  allows a small amount of rotation when flexed, which is when it is most vulnerable
  to twisting injuries (e.g. planting and pivoting).
- **Saddle/condyloid/pivot (wrist, thumb, neck's C1-C2)**: intermediate mobility with
  more constrained motion than ball-and-socket joints, generally lower injury rates in
  training contexts than the shoulder, knee, or hip.

## Planes of motion

Movements are described relative to three anatomical planes: **sagittal** (forward/
back, e.g. squat, running stride), **frontal** (side-to-side, e.g. lateral lunge,
jumping jack), and **transverse** (rotational, e.g. torso rotation in a golf swing or
throw). Most training programs are sagittal-plane-dominant (squat, hinge, press,
pull), which is efficient for building strength but leaves frontal- and
transverse-plane strength underdeveloped — a common contributor to injuries that occur
during cutting, pivoting, or lateral movements in field/court sports, since the
tissues involved were never trained to handle load in that plane.

## Major muscle groups and their primary roles

- **Gluteus maximus**: primary hip extensor; drives sprinting, jumping, and standing
  up from a squat or deadlift. Weakness here is commonly implicated in lower-back and
  knee pain because other muscles compensate for hip-extension force they aren't well
  suited to provide.
- **Gluteus medius/minimus (hip abductors)**: stabilize the pelvis in single-leg
  stance (running, single-leg squats). Weakness allows the opposite hip to drop and
  the working knee to drift inward (dynamic knee valgus), a pattern linked to
  IT band syndrome and patellofemoral pain (see `running-injury-prevention.md`,
  training domain).
- **Quadriceps**: knee extensors, also cross the hip (rectus femoris) to assist hip
  flexion. Primary shock absorbers on landing and downhill running.
  Hamstrings: hip extensors and knee flexors; eccentric hamstring strength is the
  single most evidence-backed protective factor against hamstring strain injuries in
  sprinting-based sports.
- **Erector spinae and deep core (transverse abdominis, multifidus)**: the
  transverse abdominis and multifidus provide segmental spinal stability rather than
  producing large movements; training them (e.g. planks, dead bugs, bird dogs)
  supports spine position under load more directly than pure "ab strength" from
  flexion-based exercises like crunches.
- **Rotator cuff (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis)**:
  dynamically stabilizes the humeral head in the shoulder socket during arm movement;
  distinct from the larger deltoid, which produces most of the arm's movement force
  but does little to keep the joint centered.

## Tendon, ligament, and cartilage — what each does and why it matters for training

- **Tendon** connects muscle to bone and transmits contractile force; it adapts to
  load more slowly than muscle (weeks to months vs. days to weeks), which is why
  tendon injuries are common when training load increases faster than tendons can
  remodel, even if the muscles themselves feel ready.
- **Ligament** connects bone to bone and provides joint stability; unlike muscle,
  ligaments have limited blood supply and heal more slowly and with less capacity to
  fully regain pre-injury strength, especially after a complete tear.
- **Cartilage** (articular cartilage on joint surfaces, or fibrocartilage like the
  knee's meniscus or hip's labrum) cushions and distributes joint load; it has little
  to no blood supply, heals very slowly if at all, and its condition is a major reason
  degenerative joint pain (e.g. osteoarthritis) doesn't resolve the way a muscle strain
  does — management for cartilage-related pain focuses on load management and
  surrounding muscular support rather than expecting the tissue itself to fully repair.

## The kinetic chain concept

Force in athletic movement transfers through a chain of joints and tissues (e.g.
ground → ankle → knee → hip → trunk → shoulder in a throw), and a limitation anywhere
in the chain — a stiff ankle, a weak glute, limited thoracic rotation — often shows up
as pain or breakdown at a *different* joint that has to compensate. This is why
persistent knee or shoulder pain that doesn't respond to treating the knee or shoulder
directly often resolves once a deficit elsewhere in the chain (commonly hip strength
or ankle/thoracic mobility) is addressed instead.
