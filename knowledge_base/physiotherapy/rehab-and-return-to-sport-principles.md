# Rehab and Return-to-Sport Principles

## Immediate management: PEACE & LOVE (replacing RICE)

Sports-medicine guidance has largely moved on from RICE (Rest, Ice, Compression,
Elevation) toward frameworks that better reflect the healing process:

- **PEACE** (first days): **P**rotect the area from further damage, **E**levate it,
  **A**void anti-inflammatory modalities and unnecessary rest that could blunt
  healing signaling, **C**ompress to limit swelling, **E**ducate the person that most
  soft-tissue injuries heal well with an active approach rather than prolonged
  passive rest.
- **LOVE** (after the first few days): **L**oad the tissue progressively as
  symptoms allow, stay **O**ptimistic (expectation and mindset measurably affect
  recovery outcomes), maintain **V**ascularisation (light cardiovascular activity
  that doesn't aggravate the injury, to support blood flow and healing), and
  **E**xercise through a progressive, pain-guided loading program.

Complete rest beyond the first few days is rarely the right default for a
soft-tissue injury; the exception is a suspected fracture, complete tear, or any
finding in `medical-red-flags-requiring-referral.md`, which needs medical evaluation
before any loading.

## Progressive loading is the core of most rehab

Nearly all evidence-based rehab protocols (tendinopathy, muscle strain, post-sprain,
post-surgical once cleared) follow the same underlying logic: apply the *minimum*
tissue load that stimulates adaptation without exceeding what the tissue can
currently tolerate, then increase that load in small, monitored steps. A common
pain-monitoring guideline used in tendon and muscle rehab is to allow exercise pain
up to about 3-4/10 that settles back to baseline within 24 hours; pain that spikes
higher or lingers past 24 hours signals the load progressed faster than the tissue
could handle, and the next session's load should be reduced rather than pushed
through.

## Typical rehab progression stages

1. **Symptom control / protection**: reduce pain and swelling enough to tolerate
   gentle movement; avoid the specific aggravating motion, not all motion.
2. **Restore range of motion**: regain pain-free joint motion before adding
   significant load — loading a joint that hasn't regained motion often reinforces
   compensation patterns.
3. **Restore strength**: progressive resistance work, typically starting isometric
   (no joint movement) for very irritable tissue, then progressing to full-range
   dynamic strengthening.
4. **Restore movement quality and power**: reintroduce sport-specific movement
   patterns (change of direction, jumping/landing mechanics, sprint mechanics) once
   basic strength is restored, since strength alone doesn't guarantee the
   coordination needed to tolerate sport demands safely.
5. **Return to sport**: graded reintroduction to full training and competition (see
   below), not an immediate jump back to pre-injury volume/intensity.

## Return-to-sport criteria (not calendar time)

Common, evidence-supported criteria before clearing a return to full sport,
regardless of how much time has passed:

- Pain-free through full range of motion and with sport-specific movements at
  competition intensity, not just daily activities.
- Strength within roughly 90% of the uninjured limb on formal or informal testing
  (a persistent asymmetry is one of the more consistent predictors of re-injury,
  particularly after ACL and hamstring injuries).
- Full confidence in the limb/area during unplanned, reactive movement (cutting,
  landing) — hesitation or guarding under sport-like unpredictability is a sign
  psychological readiness hasn't caught up with tissue healing, which itself
  predicts re-injury risk.
- A structured, graded return protocol (e.g. 25% → 50% → 75% → 100% of
  competition volume/intensity over multiple sessions) rather than a single
  all-or-nothing return, mirroring the return-to-running guidance in
  `running-injury-prevention.md` (training domain).

## What manual therapy can and can't do

Manual therapy (massage, joint mobilization, manipulation, dry needling) can produce
short-term pain relief and improved range of motion, which is useful for creating a
window to load tissue more comfortably. Evidence does not support manual therapy
alone producing durable structural change or strength gains — it works best as an
adjunct that enables active, progressive-loading rehab, not as a substitute for it.
An athlete who receives only passive treatment (massage, stim, ultrasound) for weeks
without a loading program is likely to see symptoms return once treatment stops.
