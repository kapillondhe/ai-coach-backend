# Common Tendon and Joint Conditions

Running-specific lower-limb overuse injuries (IT band syndrome, plantar fasciitis,
shin splints, patellofemoral pain) are covered in `running-injury-prevention.md`
(training domain). This file covers common conditions in strength training and
general sport that aren't running-specific.

## Achilles tendinopathy

Gradual-onset ache or stiffness in the Achilles tendon, classically worst with the
first steps in the morning and easing with light activity before returning after
rest — a pattern typical of tendinopathy rather than acute injury (see
`injury-classification-and-tissue-healing.md`). Two subtypes: **mid-portion**
(2-6cm above the heel, most common, often linked to sudden increases in running or
jumping volume) and **insertional** (at the heel bone itself, often aggravated by
deep ankle dorsiflexion stretching, which should generally be avoided or minimized
for this subtype unlike mid-portion tendinopathy). Management centers on progressive
calf-tendon loading (isometric holds progressing to heavy slow resistance training
like weighted calf raises) over many weeks to months, not rest — tendinopathy
recovery is typically slower than most muscle injuries. Sudden sharp pain with a
"pop" and inability to push off suggests a rupture and needs urgent evaluation, not
a rehab program.

## Lateral and medial epicondylitis ("tennis elbow" and "golfer's elbow")

Pain at the outer (lateral) or inner (medial) elbow where the wrist extensor or
flexor tendons attach, aggravated by gripping and repetitive wrist motion — despite
the names, most cases in the general population come from repetitive occupational or
training-related wrist loading (e.g. heavy grip work, repetitive rowing/pulling),
not racquet sports specifically. Like Achilles tendinopathy, this is usually a
degenerative tendinopathy rather than acute inflammation, and responds best to
progressive eccentric/isometric wrist-tendon loading combined with temporarily
reducing the aggravating grip volume, rather than rest alone.

## Rotator cuff tendinopathy and impingement

Covered from an exercise-selection angle in `shoulder-safe-substitutions.md`
(exercises domain); clinically this presents as pain with overhead reaching or
lying on the affected shoulder, often with a painful arc between roughly 60-120
degrees of arm elevation that eases above and below that range. Most cases in
recreational lifters and overhead athletes are tendinopathy/impingement-pattern pain
responsive to rotator-cuff and scapular strengthening over 6-12 weeks. Sudden acute
weakness (inability to hold the arm up against light resistance), numbness, or a
traumatic mechanism (e.g. a fall onto an outstretched arm) suggests a possible acute
rotator cuff tear and warrants medical evaluation before continuing a
strengthening-only approach.

## Swimmer's shoulder

A rotator cuff tendinopathy/impingement presentation (see above) specific to
swimmers, driven by the sheer repetition of overhead rotation — competitive
distance swimmers accumulate tens of thousands of strokes per week, far more
repetitive shoulder loading than most other overhead sports. Reported in a
large proportion of competitive swimmers at some point in their career, most
often in freestyle and butterfly, which load the shoulder through the largest
overhead range at the highest frequency. Common contributing factors, several
of which are addressable without stopping swimming entirely:

- **Technique faults** that increase shoulder strain, particularly a dropped
  elbow on the catch or a crossover entry past the body's centerline (see
  `swimming-warmup-and-drills.md` for both) — correcting technique is often as
  important as rest or strengthening in resolving symptoms.
- **A sharp increase in weekly swim volume or added paddle/equipment use**
  without a proportional buildup, mirroring the overuse pattern behind most
  tendinopathies (see `injury-classification-and-tissue-healing.md`).
- **Underdeveloped scapular stability and rotator cuff strength** relative to
  the demand of high-volume overhead swimming — dryland scapular and
  rotator-cuff work (see `swimming-warmup-and-drills.md` and
  `swim-training-plan.md`) is one of the more consistently evidence-backed
  preventive measures.

Management follows the same rotator-cuff-tendinopathy approach above
(progressive rotator-cuff and scapular strengthening over 6-12 weeks), usually
alongside a temporary reduction in swim volume or intensity — most cases
respond to modified training rather than requiring a full stop — plus the
technique corrections above so the same aggravating pattern isn't simply
reinforced once volume returns. The same acute red flags apply: sudden
traumatic onset, marked weakness, or numbness warrants medical evaluation
before continuing a strengthening-only approach.

## Low back pain (mechanical vs. needing referral)

The large majority of low back pain in athletes is "non-specific" or mechanical —
related to movement patterns and load tolerance rather than a specific structural
diagnosis — and improves with continued (modified) activity, not prolonged bed rest.
It typically varies with position and movement (better or worse with specific
postures) and doesn't radiate past the knee. Refer for medical evaluation rather
than self-managing when back pain is accompanied by: pain that is constant and
unrelated to position or movement, unexplained weight loss, fever, pain that
worsens at night, or any of the neurological red flags in
`medical-red-flags-requiring-referral.md` (especially saddle numbness or new bowel/
bladder changes, which need emergency evaluation).

## Ankle sprains

The large majority are **lateral** (inversion) sprains, injuring the anterior
talofibular ligament first and most severely. Even Grade I-II lateral ankle sprains
benefit from early protected weight-bearing and progressive loading (per
`rehab-and-return-to-sport-principles.md`) over prolonged immobilization, which
tends to produce worse long-term outcomes and higher rates of chronic ankle
instability. Because up to ~1 in 3 people have persistent instability or re-sprain
risk after an initial ankle sprain, balance/proprioception training (single-leg
balance progressions) is a standard, evidence-backed part of rehab even after
symptoms resolve, not an optional add-on.

## Hamstring strain

Most commonly occurs during high-speed running (sprinting, sudden acceleration)
rather than stretching-type mechanisms, typically at the muscle-tendon junction of
biceps femoris. Prior hamstring strain is the single strongest risk factor for a
future one, and eccentric hamstring strength (e.g. Nordic curls) is the most
consistently evidence-backed protective exercise. Return-to-sprint progression
should be criteria-based (pain-free at progressively higher running speeds) rather
than time-based, since re-injury risk is highest in the first 2-8 weeks after
returning to full training.
