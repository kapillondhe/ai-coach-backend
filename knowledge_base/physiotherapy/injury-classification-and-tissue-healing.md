# Injury Classification and Tissue Healing

## Strain vs. sprain

A **strain** is an injury to muscle or tendon (e.g. a hamstring strain); a **sprain**
is an injury to a ligament (e.g. an ankle sprain). Both are graded on the same
three-tier severity scale:

- **Grade I (mild)**: microscopic fiber damage, minimal loss of strength/stability,
  mild pain, usually able to continue some activity. Typical recovery: days to ~2
  weeks.
- **Grade II (moderate)**: partial tear, noticeable loss of strength or joint
  stability, moderate-to-significant pain and swelling, activity meaningfully
  limited. Typical recovery: 2-8 weeks.
- **Grade III (severe)**: complete tear/rupture, substantial or total loss of
  function, often significant swelling and bruising. Usually requires medical
  imaging and evaluation for possible surgical repair (e.g. complete ACL or Achilles
  tendon rupture); recovery is measured in months.

Grade should be assessed by a medical professional rather than estimated from pain
level alone — pain doesn't reliably track tissue damage (a Grade III tear can be less
acutely painful than a Grade II strain once the initial injury moment passes).

## Acute injury vs. overuse injury

**Acute** injuries result from a single identifiable traumatic event (a fall, a
twist, direct contact) and typically present with a clear onset moment. **Overuse**
(repetitive-strain) injuries develop gradually from cumulative sub-maximal loading
outpacing tissue adaptation, with no single causal event — most running and
strength-training injuries are overuse injuries (see `running-injury-prevention.md`,
training domain, for running-specific examples). This distinction matters for
management: acute injuries often need initial protection then progressive loading,
while overuse injuries usually need the *causative* training-load error identified
and corrected, not just the symptomatic tissue treated, or the injury recurs once
training resumes at the same error.

## Tendinitis vs. tendinopathy/tendinosis

True inflammatory "tendinitis" is uncommon beyond the first 48-72 hours after an
acute tendon overload. Most persistent tendon pain (Achilles, patellar, rotator cuff,
elbow) is **tendinopathy** — a degenerative/failed-healing process in the tendon
structure (collagen disorganization) with little ongoing inflammation — which is why
it typically responds poorly to rest and anti-inflammatories alone and instead needs
progressive tendon loading (see `rehab-and-return-to-sport-principles.md`) to drive
structural remodeling. Persistent reliance on ice/NSAIDs beyond the acute phase for a
tendon that has been painful for weeks is a common reason tendinopathy becomes
chronic.

## The three phases of tissue healing

1. **Inflammatory phase (roughly days 0-5)**: the body clears damaged tissue and
   initiates repair; swelling, warmth, and pain are expected and functional, not
   purely negative signs. Aggressive icing or anti-inflammatory use during this
   window can blunt the signaling that drives subsequent repair, which is one reason
   current sports-medicine guidance has moved away from routine, extended NSAID/ice
   use for soft-tissue injuries in favor of short-term symptom control only.
2. **Proliferative/repair phase (roughly days 5-21)**: new (initially disorganized)
   collagen and tissue is laid down. Gentle, progressively loaded movement in this
   window helps new tissue organize along the lines of mechanical stress it will
   need to tolerate; complete immobilization for this whole window tends to produce
   weaker, stiffer repair tissue than early controlled loading does.
3. **Remodeling phase (roughly 3 weeks to several months, tissue-dependent)**:
   collagen reorganizes and strengthens toward — but often not fully back to —
   pre-injury tissue quality. Tendon and ligament remodeling is markedly slower than
   muscle remodeling, which is a major reason return-to-sport timelines for
   ligament/tendon injuries are longer than for comparable-grade muscle strains.

## Why timelines are ranges, not fixed dates

Healing rate depends on tissue type (muscle heals faster than tendon, which heals
faster than ligament, which heals faster than cartilage — see
`sports-anatomy-fundamentals.md`), blood supply to the injured area, age, overall
training load, sleep and nutrition status, and adherence to a progressive loading
program. Because of this, return-to-activity decisions should be based on
functional criteria (pain-free range of motion, strength symmetry with the
uninjured side, sport-specific movement tolerance) rather than calendar time alone —
see `rehab-and-return-to-sport-principles.md` for specific criteria.
