# Medical Red Flags Requiring Referral

This file exists to help a coach recognize when something is outside the scope of
coaching/training advice and needs a medical professional — not to diagnose or treat
any of these conditions. When any of these signs are present, the right response is
to direct the athlete to appropriate medical care (urgent/emergency care for the
first group), not to suggest exercise modifications or rehab exercises.

## Seek emergency care immediately

- **Chest pain, pressure, or tightness during exercise**, especially with shortness
  of breath, dizziness, or pain radiating to the arm/jaw — do not treat as
  exercise-induced discomfort.
- **Sudden, severe ("worst of my life") headache**, or a head impact followed by
  worsening headache, vomiting, confusion, unequal pupils, slurred speech, or loss of
  consciousness — possible concussion with warning signs, or a more serious
  intracranial event.
- **Calf pain and swelling, especially one-sided, with warmth and redness** —
  possible deep vein thrombosis (DVT); do not massage or continue exercising the leg,
  as this can dislodge a clot.
- **New saddle-area numbness, or new loss of bowel/bladder control, with back pain**
  — possible cauda equina syndrome, a surgical emergency.
- **Severe, disproportionate limb pain after an injury that worsens rather than
  improves, especially with tightness, numbness, or pallor** — possible compartment
  syndrome, a surgical emergency; do not wait to see if it settles with rest.
- **Fever with a hot, swollen, single joint** — possible septic arthritis, a medical
  emergency distinct from typical exercise-related joint pain.
- **Loss of consciousness, seizure, or ongoing confusion after a head or body
  impact**.

## Warrants prompt (non-emergency) medical evaluation before continuing training

- **Pinpoint, specific bone tenderness** that worsens with continued activity and
  doesn't ease with rest (vs. the diffuse ache of shin splints/tendinopathy) —
  possible stress fracture; see `running-injury-prevention.md` (training domain) for
  how to distinguish shin splints from a tibial stress fracture specifically.
- **A joint that feels unstable, "gives way," or won't bear weight** after an
  injury — possible ligament rupture or fracture.
- **Numbness, tingling, or weakness that persists** beyond the immediate injury
  moment, or that follows a specific nerve distribution (e.g. down one arm or leg).
- **A visible deformity, or a "pop" felt/heard at the time of injury** followed by
  rapid, significant swelling — possible complete tendon/ligament rupture or
  fracture.
- **Menstrual irregularity or loss of periods in an athlete with high training
  volume**, alongside low energy availability or disordered eating patterns —
  possible Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S), which needs medical and
  nutritional evaluation rather than a training-load fix alone.
- **Concussion symptoms** (headache, dizziness, difficulty concentrating,
  light/noise sensitivity) even without loss of consciousness — needs medical
  clearance before return to any contact or high-risk activity; return-to-play
  decisions after concussion should never be made by a coach alone.
- **Pain that is constant, unrelated to movement or position, worse at night, or
  accompanied by unexplained weight loss or fever** — atypical for a mechanical
  training injury and warrants evaluation to rule out a non-musculoskeletal cause.

## General principle for scope of coaching advice

Training and rehab guidance elsewhere in this knowledge base (see
`rehab-and-return-to-sport-principles.md`, `injury-classification-and-tissue-healing.md`)
assumes a routine, non-red-flag musculoskeletal issue. Any of the signs above
override that guidance: the appropriate response is referral to a physician,
physical therapist, or emergency care as indicated, not a training modification,
and a coach or AI assistant should not attempt to talk an athlete out of seeking
that evaluation.
