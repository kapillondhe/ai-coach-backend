Ask first: swimming background (learned as an adult or swam as a kid), current
100 m pace or CSS, how often they swim, and pool or open water.

Our position: for most adult-onset swimmers, technique limits speed far more
than fitness. More laps with poor technique just practice the mistakes.

Pool:
- Frequency first: 3 swims a week of 30-45 minutes beats 1-2 long ones for
  technique and feel for the water.
- Every session: warmup, a short drill set, a main set, cooldown. Main sets of
  repeats with short rest (for example 10 x 100 m at CSS pace + 5-10 s rest)
  build swim fitness better than continuous swimming.
- Most useful fixes for triathletes: relaxed exhale underwater (holding the
  breath causes the panic feeling), head down and still, long body line (hips
  up), high-elbow catch, no crossing over the centre line. Get a video or a
  coach's eye: people can't feel their own stroke faults.
- Drills to name: catch-up, single-arm, fist drill, kicking on the side, sculling.
  Pick 1-2 for the athlete's main fault rather than a long list.
- Pull buoy and paddles: occasionally, not as a crutch. Paddles plus high volume
  is a common cause of shoulder pain.

Open water:
- Practice it before race day: at least a few open-water swims in the build,
  ideally with others and with safety cover. Never swim alone.
- Sighting: lift the eyes just above the surface ("crocodile eyes") every 6-10
  strokes, more often in chop, then roll into the breath. Practice it in the
  pool.
- Drafting: swimming just behind another swimmer's feet or by their hip saves
  real energy. It's legal and smart.
- Wetsuit: it adds buoyancy and speed, especially for weaker swimmers. Practice
  in it, and make sure it fits tightly at the shoulders. Event rules set wetsuit
  cut-offs by water temperature; check the athlete guide.
- Cold water: acclimate over several swims. On entry, give the first minute to
  controlled breathing before swimming hard.

Mass-start anxiety (very common):
- Warm up in the water if allowed, start at the side or back of the pack, and
  let the first few hundred metres be easy.
- If panic hits: roll onto the back or switch to breaststroke, slow the breathing,
  then restart. That's normal race practice, not failure.
- Safety: chest pain, unusual breathlessness, or dizziness in the swim means
  stopping and signalling for help (roll on the back, raise a hand). Most race
  deaths in triathlon happen in the swim, so never push through these symptoms.
